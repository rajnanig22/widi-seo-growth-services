Nonprofit SEO Isn't Business SEO, Because the Audiences Don't Want the Same Thing

Widi Ginanjar · July 29, 2026 · 5 min read

Try this: pull up your organisation's homepage and ask who it's actually talking to. Not who it's supposed to serve. Who it's talking to, right now, in the words and layout you actually see.

Most nonprofit homepages have a clear answer, and it's almost always the same one: the donor. That isn't unique to nonprofits. Plenty of B2B software sites have an end-user, a budget-holder, and an internal champion all reading the same page, and those are three genuinely different people too. The difference is what happens after. In most businesses, no matter how many different people read a page, they're all working toward the same outcome: a purchase, a signup, a demo request. A nonprofit site can have a similar mix of visitors, but they don't want the same outcome at all.

A donor wants to know if the organisation is credible and where the money goes. A beneficiary, or their family, wants to know how to actually get the help. A funder or grantmaker wants proof of track record before committing anything.

These aren't three versions of the same question. They're three different people who want three different outcomes, and each one will leave unsatisfied if the page doesn't address them specifically. Nielsen Norman Group's usability testing across 60 nonprofit sites found homepages consistently under-serving one audience or another, since most were built with a single visitor in mind rather than the several who actually arrive. When a nonprofit's whole site is quietly built around one of them, usually the donor, the other two don't just get less attention. They often get none.

What "built for one audience" looks like in practice

I looked at two real, unrelated nonprofit homepages with exactly this question in mind. Each one had the problem, just in opposite directions.

One was an environmental NGO I used to work for. The homepage is, honestly, well done: worker profiles with real names and real stories, an impact-numbers section, partner testimonials from cities they've worked with. Everything about it builds trust in how the money is used. But the organisation's actual work is partnering with cities and companies, and if you're a city official or a business lead wondering how that partnership starts, there's nothing on the homepage that addresses you at all. The trust-building is strong. The answer to "how do I actually begin" doesn't exist.

The second was a small NGO running a safe house and education program in Jakarta. Same problem, opposite direction. The homepage has a whole section built around donating and volunteering, with a large, clearly visible donate button. But the pages describing the actual programs, the safe house, the school, only describe what the organisation does. They don't say how to contact anyone or ask for help. Nothing tells a family who needs that safe house tonight how to actually reach someone. And the one page that would matter most to a grantmaker doing due diligence, anything with real outcomes or a track record, sits two clicks away under a menu called "Publication," and isn't visible from the homepage at all.

Neither site is badly built. Both are simply built for one visitor: whichever one the team had in mind when they wrote the homepage.

Why this matters more than it looks like

This isn't really a technical SEO problem, in the sense of broken tags or crawl errors. It's a content architecture problem, and it changes which pages and keywords are even worth building. A business can usually optimize a page toward one converting action, no matter how many personas read it. A nonprofit page often can't, because "how do I get a grant," "how do I donate," and "how do I get help" aren't different paths to the same outcome. They're different outcomes. A site built to answer only one of them will struggle to rank, or convert, for the other two, no matter how technically clean the pages are.

The fix isn't building three separate sites. Most nonprofits, especially small ones running on a volunteer-managed site, don't have the resources for that. It's simpler than it sounds: figure out which of the three audiences your current site is quietly ignoring, and give that one audience a single clear page, or even just a clear paragraph, that actually answers their question.

Here's the fast way to check. Open your homepage and time yourself: 10 seconds to find "how do I donate," 10 seconds to find "how do people get help," 10 seconds to find real proof of results. If one of those takes longer than the other two, or doesn't exist at all, that's your answer. Most people who run the site have never timed it, because they already know where everything is. That's exactly why it's easy to miss.

The gap is usually easier to spot from the outside than from the inside, which is part of why it goes unnoticed for so long.
