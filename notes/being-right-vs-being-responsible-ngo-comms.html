Being Right About the Data Isn't the Same as Being Responsible With It

Widi Ginanjar · SEO & Growth · September 5, 2026 · 5 min read

The bottom line: a colleague told me the last line of a post I wrote sounded passive-aggressive. My first move was to defend it, since the numbers behind it were solid. It took me a while to see the argument was never really about the numbers. It was three different questions, all squeezed into one sentence, and no rewording was going to fix that.

The post that started it

The post was about forest fires. It rested on one fact I had checked twice: most of the fire hotspots in that region sat on land held by companies, not on land worked by small farmers. That's a real, checkable pattern. It also goes against the usual story about dry season and small-scale land clearing.

The trouble was the ending. The draft closed with a question aimed straight at that fact, something like who should be held responsible. A colleague flagged it. Not the data, just that last question. He felt it repeated an accusation the post had already made once, just said again in a sharper way.

My first instinct was to push back a little. The numbers were right, so why did the tone matter so much? Writing this out is what made me see it. Three separate things were tangled up in that one line, and only one of them was really about wording.

When impact stories start to feel hollow

Mission-driven content leans hard on numbers: hectares restored, households reached, tons of carbon stored somewhere. Those numbers are real and worth sharing. But a feed that only ever says look what we did starts to feel like it hasn't noticed how big the problem still is. I've felt this as a reader too, scrolling past a nonprofit account that's all wins and no friction, and wondering if anyone there has actually looked at the scale of what they're up against.

When sharper framing starts to feel like an accusation

The other extreme, leaning hard into the uncomfortable data, has its own version of this problem. Naming exactly where the fires were was just accurate reporting. Turning that fact into a pointed question about blame was a separate choice, added on top. That's the part that actually caused friction. The data didn't need defending. What I built on top of it did.

The part that took the longest to admit

Here's the part I didn't want to say out loud at first: the organisation behind this post also works with government partners in the same area the post was, in a roundabout way, criticising through a line about shrinking disaster-response budgets. That's not a small detail. There's real research on this. A 2012 study on nonprofits that depend on government funding found that these groups tend to avoid public confrontation and lean into quieter, cooperative approaches instead, mostly to protect their standing as a trusted partner. This isn't just one country's problem either. A recent US survey found that nearly two in three nonprofit communications staff now worry about government pushback or losing funding over their advocacy work, and close to half worry about backlash over sensitive topics.

I don't think that means the budget line should have been softened into nothing. It means my hesitation wasn't just nerves. It's a real trade-off a lot of people in this line of work run into, whether they ever put a name to it or not.

What actually changed in the end

We kept the hard parts. The statistic about the fires stayed. The line about the budgets stayed. What changed was the last line. Instead of asking who should be held responsible, the post ended on the organisation's own work: smaller in scale, but built on the same idea. The question aimed at someone else got cut. The line about what we're actually doing stayed.

It's a smaller fix than it probably sounds. It didn't solve the bigger tension between wanting the post to make people think and not wanting to damage a working relationship it was standing right next to. It just meant I stopped trying to solve three problems with one closing sentence.

Why I'm writing this down

People talk about bold and safe like two ends of one dial, and I don't think that's quite right. They're really answers to three different questions. Does this content actually show the size of the problem. Does the framing make people think, or does it just point a finger. And does this organisation's own position, who it works with, who it depends on, change what it can say in public without hurting itself. Squashing all three into "should we tone this down" is how good feedback ends up sounding like someone just getting cold feet.
